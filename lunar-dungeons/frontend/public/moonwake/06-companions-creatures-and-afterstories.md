# The Moonwake Testament
## Companion folios, creature histories, and the lives after the five notes

This volume deepens the people and small lives encountered across the five acts.
It is supplementary canon: these stories enrich the journey without changing its established route.
The creatures are not all enemies.
The companions are not rewards.
The bestiary is a record of how to understand a living thing, not merely how to defeat it.

---

## I. The people who kept walking

### Auralis, who became Iri

Auralis grew up beneath the western dome of Moonwake.
Her family repaired small things that other people thought were too ordinary to save.
They mended cracked cups.
They replaced loose hinges.
They returned lost buttons to the coat that had dropped them.
Auralis learned that a home was made from small acts repeated with care.
She became an archivist because she loved the ordinary detail of a life.
She catalogued street cats, rain patterns, festival songs, and the names people chose for their gardens.
She did not believe that every memory belonged in a book.
She believed a book could help someone find a memory they had chosen to keep.
When the Quiet began to gather, Auralis noticed that the archive had become a map.
The Quiet travelled along names that had been recorded without permission.
She removed her own name from the public catalogue.
She placed it in the lantern because the lantern could remember without displaying.
The act saved the archive for a time.
It also left her unsure how to introduce herself.
The name Iri became a small shelter.
The name Auralis remained a room she could enter when ready.
Across the journey, she learns that she is allowed to keep both.
Her final growth is not the recovery of a lost identity.
It is the freedom to decide how that identity is carried.
After the ending, she tends the observatory as a place of welcome.
She no longer records every arrival.
She asks whether a visitor wishes to leave anything behind.
Some leave a name.
Some leave a drawing.
Some leave nothing.
The door stays open.

### Vey, who kept a place by the fire

Vey was taught to work metal by a person whose face he no longer remembers.
He remembers the teacher's hands.
They warmed every tool before passing it to another worker.
They never made a tired apprentice lift a hot piece alone.
Vey learned that craftsmanship was not only the shape of an object.
It was the shape of the care that surrounded its making.
He became an ash-smith.
He learned how to distribute heat through the city.
He learned that a furnace could keep a district warm while a small hearth kept one person from feeling alone.
When Orin visited Ember Hollow, Vey promised to keep a place for anyone who arrived without a name.
He did not know whether Orin would ever return.
He did not know whether the guest would be one person.
He kept the place anyway.
His story is not about waiting forever.
It is about learning that a promise can be renewed by the people who live beside it.
After the Sovereign rests, Vey teaches the Quiet Flame to new apprentices.
He makes no apprentice work alone.
He keeps the empty chair by the hearth, but it is no longer reserved for a ghost.
It is offered to whoever needs it.

### Edda, who chose a name

Edda began as a reader of old inscriptions.
She could recognise a name by the pressure of its letters.
She believed that preserving a name was a form of protection.
When the Quiet reached Frostglass, she copied every name she could find.
She did not want another person to disappear without a trace.
The work became her life.
The library became her whole world.
She forgot that a name was carried by a living person.
She forgot that a person could change their name without becoming false.
She forgot that privacy was not the same as erasure.
The Hollow Monarch gave shape to her fear.
Its shelves were perfect.
Its people were still.
Edda's choice to use a name she had chosen was the first change in the library's law.
She did not destroy the old page.
She placed it somewhere private.
She did not demand that the new name be permanent.
She kept it in the open.
After the story, Edda teaches people how to revise their records.
She makes it possible to remove a public entry without destroying a private memory.
She writes one sentence at the entrance.
"Your name is yours before it is ours."

### Nym, who learned to travel

Nym was the youngest cartographer in the Astral Archive when the observatories first began to move.
They were fascinated by routes.
They believed every place could be reached if the map was accurate enough.
They were travelling with a friend when a bridge shifted.
Nym looked down to finish a measurement.
The friend crossed without them.
Nym never learned whether the friend arrived.
They began drawing safer bridges.
They drew more accurate routes.
They drew the same route again and again.
The map became a substitute for the journey.
Nym's grief turned into a rule: a map should never leave a traveller uncertain.
The rule could not be kept.
The Astral Warden reinforced it.
The Wayfarer gave Nym permission to leave a room blank.
Nym discovers that uncertainty can be honest.
They no longer promise that every route is safe.
They promise to mark what they know and leave room for what they do not.
After the ending, Nym travels between the five kingdoms.
They draw maps that can be changed.
They include routes people choose not to take.
They leave blank spaces for future travellers.
Their new atlas is never complete.
That is why it remains useful.

### Orin, who learned to be one person again

Orin was not born to be the First Keeper.
They became a listener because they were willing to wait.
People trusted them with unfinished stories.
Orin learned that a silence could be a request rather than an absence.
When the Lumen began to overflow, Orin tried to carry every voice.
They believed that a Keeper should be able to hold what others could not.
They did not ask the five wardens to share the work.
They did not ask Auralis whether she wanted to take the risk.
They divided the Lumen and bound the Quiet.
The choice saved the kingdoms from immediate collapse.
It also made Orin the only person allowed to hear the silence.
Their long vigil became a form of control.
They believed that if they stopped, the world would fall.
The Wayfarer shows them that protection without consent can become another kind of silence.
Orin's return is not an instant apology accepted by everyone.
Iri is allowed to remain uncertain.
The kingdoms are allowed to decide how much trust they offer.
Orin learns to help without becoming the centre.
After the ending, Orin cooks badly.
They learn to mend a chair.
They learn to sleep through a whole night.
They listen when someone asks them to stop.
They are no longer a title.
They are a person with time to live.

### The Wayfarer, who was not chosen

The Wayfarer is deliberately not given a fixed name or a secret royal history.
They arrive beneath the observatory because the story has reached a place where another voice is possible.
The Lumen does not select them as a perfect person.
It does not know whether they are brave.
It does not know whether they will make every choice well.
It simply leaves room.
The Wayfarer becomes important by choosing to listen.
They do not solve every problem with a strike.
They learn to pause before approaching a guarded thing.
They learn to leave an unfinished memory alone.
They learn that a route may be open without being compulsory.
They learn that an ending is a choice, not a reward.
After the final chapter, the Wayfarer continues travelling.
They may return to every kingdom.
They may stay away for a time.
The story does not punish them for resting.
The lantern remains a companion, not a command.

## II. The small people of the kingdoms

### Pell, gardener of the bell flowers

Pell tends the Bell Garden.
He remembers the person who taught him to let a flower finish opening.
He does not remember their name.
He keeps the page blank because that was the request.
He knows the garden has done its work when a visitor leaves with more room than they arrived with.
After the first note returns, Pell makes the garden easier to cross.
He removes the thorn barriers that were only there to frighten travellers.
He leaves the old warning bell in the earth.
He does not ring it for festivals.
It is still a warning.
He teaches younger gardeners that a warning is not a command.
It is information offered in time to choose.

### Sella, guest at the open hearth

Sella arrives at Vey's stall on a cold evening.
She has no coat suitable for the mountain wind.
She has no money for a room.
She does have a small paper star.
She places it beside the empty cup.
Vey asks whether she wishes to sit.
She says yes.
He does not ask where she came from.
She warms her hands.
The paper star dries beside the flame.
Later, Sella returns with a second cup.
She places it beside the first.
The promise has become a custom.
The city learns that an open seat can be used without being claimed.

### The bridge workers

The bridge workers maintain Slag Bridge.
They disagree about the best way to repair a chain.
They argue loudly.
They share tools.
They stop when someone is tired.
One worker paints a small line on the wall where the mountain cracked.
Another paints a second line beside it.
The lines show where the repair begins and ends.
The Wayfarer sees that the bridge is safer because the workers can admit what is broken.

### The frost readers

The frost readers help people revise their names in the library.
They do not write a name unless the person asks.
They never ask why someone changed a name.
They keep the private room warm.
They make certain that the mirror can be covered.
They teach the younger archivists that a record is useful only while it serves the person it describes.

### The letter-carriers of the Archive

The letter-carriers travel between observatories.
They carry letters without reading them.
They carry maps without deciding the destination.
They carry blank paper as often as sealed envelopes.
They do not promise that every letter will arrive.
They promise to tell the sender when a route changes.
Their work helps Nym understand that a map can guide without controlling.

## III. Bestiary folios — Creatures of the living realms

### Mossling

A mossling is a small root-creature that protects young growth.
It circles a stranger before deciding whether to approach.
Its spring is a warning, not a hunt.
If the visitor steps back, the mossling often settles.
It likes damp soil and quiet shade.
Its back grows moss that changes colour with the season.
The best way to pass a mossling is to let it choose the distance.

### Thorn Sprite

A thorn sprite guards fragile pages and young branches.
It flashes before diving.
The flash marks the direction of its movement.
It is attracted to hands that reach too quickly.
It is calmed by patience and a lantern held low.
It does not understand ownership.
It understands care.

### Cinder Wisp

A cinder wisp lives near warm stone.
It circles twice before dropping a harmless but startling wave of heat.
It is drawn to places where fires are unattended.
It becomes calmer when the fire is shared.
Its glow is brightest just before dawn in Ember Hollow.

### Ash Hound

An ash hound patrols damaged bridges and furnace vents.
It leaves a trail of warm cinders.
It lowers its head before a straight lunge.
It is more concerned with the broken object than the traveller.
If the object is repaired, the hound often leaves.
It keeps a den beneath the Slag Bridge.

### Frost Echo

A frost echo repeats the last movement it sees.
It disappears in a cold shimmer and reappears behind the previous position.
It does not understand that the world has changed.
It follows the image rather than the person.
It is safest to move after the shimmer, not toward it.

### Glacier Warden

A glacier warden protects the supports of the Glass Causeway.
It raises a shield before sending a low wave along the ice.
The shield is a warning sign.
The warden does not want the bridge to collapse.
Its strongest instinct is to keep the crossing open.

### Star Construct

A star construct draws a line before releasing a pulse.
The line marks the area it intends to cross.
It is built to maintain observatory routes.
It does not pursue a traveller beyond its assigned orbit.
A changed route can confuse it.
A new chart can teach it.

### Astral Drake

An astral drake follows old flight paths.
It folds its wings before diving toward the place where a traveller stood.
It is not malicious.
It is following a route that no longer fits the room.
It can be redirected by a new star chart.

### Quietling

A quietling gathers where many voices have been left unheard.
It dims and reappears at a distance.
Its wave is slow.
It does not chase someone who waits.
A quietling often leaves a small stone that holds a sound without a name.

### Eclipse Shade

An eclipse shade repeats a delayed movement.
It leaves an echo where it intends to move next.
It can be avoided by watching the mark rather than the creature.
It appears in Echo Court where memories have been carried without a witness.

### The Unwritten Warden

The Unwritten Warden guards the margin beneath Moonwake.
It changes platforms and paths to test whether a traveller believes the old map must remain fixed.
It has no face because it has no assigned identity.
It is not a final enemy.
It is the keeper of the space where the next story may begin.

## IV. Six companion threads — The longer versions

### The Unlit Lantern

Iri's first thread begins in the observatory.
The lantern is familiar, but its purpose is not.
The Wayfarer can trust Iri with it or keep it close.
Either choice is respected.
The thread asks whether trust must be proven by surrendering control.
The answer is no.
Trust grows when both people can say what they need.

### The Bell Kept Quiet

The Bell Garden's warning has been held beneath the roots.
The Wayfarer can let the roots finish the sentence or carry the bell's note forward.
Both choices preserve the warning.
One leaves it with the forest.
The other carries it into the next kingdom.
Pell explains that a warning remains useful only while someone can hear it.

### A Hearth for Two

Vey's empty seat is a promise to an unknown guest.
The Wayfarer can keep it open or return the ember to the city.
Keeping it open teaches the city to welcome someone it cannot predict.
Returning it teaches the city to share the warmth more widely.
The empty place is not wasted in either path.
It has become a custom rather than a shrine.

### The Name Without Ink

Edda's old name is kept in a private drawer.
The Wayfarer can speak her chosen name with her or let her decide when to remember.
The thread is not about recovering a perfect past.
It is about permission.
Edda becomes more confident when the library stops demanding a single answer.

### The Blank Constellation

Nym's final star is deliberately unmarked.
The Wayfarer can leave it blank or draw a route for another traveller.
The first choice honours uncertainty.
The second choice offers guidance without pretending to guarantee an outcome.
Nym learns that an unfinished map can be complete enough to use.

### A Name for the Light

Iri remembers Auralis.
The Wayfarer can tell her the name or ask her to choose.
The first choice shares a recovered truth.
The second gives Iri ownership of the decision.
She keeps both names.
The lantern holds the sound without demanding that she choose one forever.

## V. Small side stories that can be found between rooms

### The cup that waited in Ember Hollow

A cup was left on a windowsill for a traveller who never arrived.
The cup was repaired three times.
No one knew whether the traveller was alive.
The household kept the cup because the promise was not a demand.
One winter, a child borrowed it.
The child returned it clean.
The household made another cup.
The window remained open.

### The page that Edda did not read

A page arrived at the Name Library with no writing.
Edda almost catalogued it as empty.
Then she noticed a faint pressure where a hand had rested.
She placed it in the private room.
No one asked to read it.
Years later, the page was returned to the person who had left it.
The person smiled.
They did not explain.

### The star that Nym removed

Nym once removed a star from the atlas because it no longer matched its recorded position.
The star had moved.
The atlas had not.
Nym thought the star was wrong.
After the Warden's release, Nym placed the star back.
They drew a new line.
The old map remained as a record of how the sky had changed.

### The lantern-moth's return

A lantern-moth left the observatory and travelled through all five kingdoms.
It rested beside Pell's bell.
It warmed its wings near Vey's hearth.
It landed on Edda's private mirror.
It followed Nym's moving atlas.
At last it returned to Iri.
The moth did not carry a message.
Its journey was the message.

### The repaired bridge

A bridge worker painted a line across a repaired chain.
A second worker painted a line beside it.
A third worker added a small star.
The marks showed where the bridge had once broken.
The workers refused to cover them.
They wanted travellers to know that the bridge was maintained, not perfect.
The bridge became safer because its repairs remained visible.

### The name on the door

After the ending, a new door appeared in the observatory.
No one knew who had built it.
It had a small plate beside the handle.
The plate was blank.
Visitors could write a name.
They could also leave it untouched.
The door was never locked.

## VI. The companions after the ending

The companions do not stop living when the Lumen is restored.
Iri keeps the observatory lantern in the front window and welcomes visitors without recording their names.
Vey teaches a new circle of ash-smiths to build smaller fires and leave time for rest.
Edda makes the Name Library a place where every person controls their own record.
Nym publishes a movable atlas with routes that can be revised and blank spaces that remain intentional.
Orin learns ordinary work again: cooking, repairing chairs, listening, apologising, and sleeping through the night.
The Wayfarer continues beyond the five kingdoms, carrying the lantern as a companion rather than a command.
Some evenings they return to the observatory.
Some evenings they do not.
The road remains open either way.
